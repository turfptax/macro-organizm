How much governments, households and companies owe, what the interest on it costs, and whether the economy is growing faster than the interest rate. Every figure is a published measurement with its source and date. This page does not say how much debt is too much; economists disagree about that, and both sides are shown.

The economies are the largest advanced economies plus China, chosen for data coverage and not as a representative sample. Low-debt advanced economies such as Australia, Switzerland or Norway are not included. The page starts with the UK, US, Japan, Italy and Canada; Germany, France and the euro area can be added, and the grey lines show the rest.

On any chart, point or touch to read the values for one year. With a keyboard, focus the chart and use the arrow keys (Shift for a year at a time, Home and End to jump, Escape to close). Every chart has a table and a CSV of the same numbers.

Notes under each chart are tagged Accounting fact (a definition or an identity), Measured trend (computed from the data on the page) or Interpretation (a reading people dispute, with the strongest case on each side).

Notes, definitions and limits

What each measure counts, where a series changes basis, and what the page leaves out.

Definitions (accounting facts)

  • Gross and net debt. Gross debt is everything a government owes, including amounts owed to other parts of government. Net debt subtracts the financial assets the government holds. This page shows gross debt only.
  • Central and general government. Central government is the national treasury. General government adds regional and local governments and, in some countries, social security funds. The IMF historical series uses general government where it has it and central government otherwise, so some series change coverage part-way.
  • Credit. The BIS total credit series counts loans and debt securities owed by households, non-financial companies and government to all lenders, at home and abroad. Households and companies are at market value, government at face (nominal) value. Debts of banks and other financial firms, and promises such as pensions, are outside it.
  • Primary balance. Government revenue minus spending other than interest. A positive number is a surplus before interest; a negative one is a deficit before interest. It is the part of the budget that does not depend on the interest rate.
  • Fiscal and calendar years. US federal figures follow the fiscal year that ends on 30 September and are keyed by the year in which it ends. They are divided only by series of the same fiscal year. Other economies use calendar years.
  • Implied interest rate (r). For the IMF and Eurostat ratios, interest paid and debt are both shares of GDP of their own year, so r = interest(t) / debt(t-1) x (1 + g), where g is nominal GDP growth in year t. For the United States federal basis, r is net interest outlays in fiscal year t divided by debt held by the public at the end of fiscal year t-1, both in dollars.
  • r minus g and the debt ratio. To a good approximation, the change in debt as a share of GDP in a year is (r - g) / (1 + g) x debt(t-1), minus the primary balance (a surplus counts as positive). Real data also contain other financing and valuation changes, so this identity and the actual change can differ.
  • Averages. A 5-year average is the mean of five consecutive years and is left blank if any of the five is missing. A line that breaks means the source has no value for those years; nothing is filled in.

    Notes on the data

      What this page does not show

      • Net debt, the assets a government holds, pension and other unfunded promises, and guarantees.
      • Forecasts. Values after the last actual year in each IMF source are removed from the file, and the outlook database is drawn only to its last actual year. The IMF feed does not flag projections, so the last actual year is taken as the year before the vintage year, and the newest year may still be an estimate.
      • A debt threshold. No level of debt is agreed to be the point where trouble starts, and the page draws none.
      • Debts of banks and other financial firms, which are a different part of the balance sheet from the sectors above.
      • Who holds the debt. Every debt is also someone's asset: households, pension funds, banks, central banks and foreign investors.
